Method

How can a ring give you a calendar year?

A tree adds one ring a year, and its width follows the weather. Many trees in one region share the same run of wide and narrow rings, so an undated timber can be matched against dated ones. The match fixes each ring to a calendar year; with the bark edge intact, the last ring is the felling year.

In a softwood like the one pictured, each ring is a pair of bands. Earlywood grows in spring and is pale, with wide, thin-walled cells. Latewood grows later in the season and is darker, denser and narrower. The sharp edge between one year’s latewood and the next year’s earlywood is the line that gets counted.

Width is the signal. A warm, wet summer lets a tree lay down a wide ring; a drought or a late frost leaves a narrow one. Trees in one region get the same weather, so their narrow and wide rings fall in the same years. Matching those patterns between samples is called crossdating, and it is the whole method. Chronologies built this way now run past 12,000 years.

The photograph on this page holds no date, so we can count its rings but not name their years. We counted 38 along the line from the pith to the top edge of the frame, with software that follows each ring outward, and checked the count against a second method. On the four cleanest rays the two agreed to within one ring.

What a sample needs

What does a timber have to show before it can be dated?

It needs a long enough run of rings to match, and ideally the bark edge. With the bark edge, the last ring gives the felling year. With some sapwood left, we can give a range. With none, the result is only a date after which the tree was felled. We say which of the three you have.

In words
The bark edgeThe felling year, from the last ring. If that ring is incomplete, often the season of felling too.
Part of the sapwoodA range for the felling date. Published sapwood estimates for English oak run from about 9 to 46 rings, depending on region and period.
Heartwood onlyA “felled after” date: the last ring plus the minimum sapwood allowance. The real felling can be decades later.

A long run of rings matches with more confidence than a short one. Reports from English Heritage’s Ancient Monuments Laboratory, held by the Archaeology Data Service, caution against relying on a single timber with fewer than 100 rings. The timber page goes through what a result states.

Material

What kinds of wood can be dated?

Any wood with clear annual rings, from a species and region that has a reference chronology. That covers roof and floor timbers, oak panels and chests, ship timbers and the spruce tops of violins. Tropical woods without clear annual rings, and species with no regional reference, are much harder.

  • Roof and floor timbersOften several from one building, which gives a firmer date.
  • Oak panels and chestsThe surface is often the only edge there is to read.
  • Ship timbersLarge pieces can carry long runs of rings.
  • Violin topsSpruce with narrow, even rings that suit measuring.

Reference records are public. NOAA’s International Tree-Ring Data Bank holds ring-width, density and isotope records from more than 5,000 sites on six continents. A chronology only helps for the species and the region it covers, so the first question about any sample is where the tree grew. We would rather give a plain “cannot be dated” than a date that does not hold.

Four more questions

Does tree-ring dating damage the timber?
Usually very little. Where a cut end is exposed, the surface can be sanded and read in place. Otherwise a small core is taken: hand borers for living trees take cores about 5 millimetres across, and the tree normally seals the hole. For a carved or painted object we would first ask whether an exposed edge already exists.
Why do you count from the bark inward?
The bark edge is the one ring with a known year: the last the tree grew. Each ring inside it is one year earlier, so counting back from it keeps every date anchored. Reference chronologies are built the same way, from living trees back through older and older timber that overlaps them.
How far back can tree-ring dating reach?
Some regional chronologies run back more than 12,000 years, built by overlapping living trees, dead trees and timber from buildings. Whether your sample can use one depends on its species and where it grew. No single chronology covers every wood or every place, so some timbers cannot be dated at all.
Can you tell a tree’s age from a stump?
Counting rings on a stump gives the tree’s age at that height. It falls a few years short of the true age, because the sapling needed time to grow that tall. A core taken nearer the ground gets closer. Neither gives a calendar date unless the rings are also matched against dated wood.

Ask about a sample

Tell us what the timber is, where it came from and whether any bark edge or pale sapwood survives. A photograph of the cut end, with a ruler beside it, is the best first step.
